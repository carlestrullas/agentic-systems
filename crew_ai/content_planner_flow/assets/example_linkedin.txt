# LinkedIn post 1

Your model's 95% accuracy might be meaningless.

Here's why.

Imagine a dataset where only 5% of the transactions are fraudulent.

A model that always predicts "not fraud" scores 95% accuracy.

It also catches exactly zero fraud.

This is the accuracy trap, and it shows up everywhere data is imbalanced: fraud, churn, medical diagnosis, defects in manufacturing.

What to look at instead:
↳ Precision: how many of the flagged cases are real
↳ Recall: how many of the real cases you flagged
↳ PR-AUC: how the trade-off behaves across thresholds

And one more thing: choose the decision threshold based on business cost, not on the default 0.5.

A metric is only useful if it reflects the cost of being wrong.

What metric do you report to stakeholders for imbalanced problems?

# LinkedIn post 2

Most data science projects don't fail because of the model.

They fail before the first line of modelling code.

After several projects in production, the pattern is always the same:

1. The problem is defined in technical terms, not business terms.
2. Nobody agrees on how success will be measured.
3. The data available at prediction time is not the data used in training.

None of these are solved with a better algorithm.

They're solved with a 30-minute conversation at the start:
→ What decision will this model support?
→ What does a wrong prediction cost?
→ What data will we really have when we need the prediction?

Ask these three questions before you open a notebook.
